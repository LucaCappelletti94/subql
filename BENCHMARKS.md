# Benchmarks

Empirical push-vs-polling CDC measurements for subql, summarized from runs on `commons_server` (64 cores, Postgres 16 + wal2json). The benchmark programs that produced these numbers are not committed; the findings below are the durable record.

## Push vs polling at scale (single PostgreSQL)

Run with 3 trials per cell, fresh source per trial, sub-1% run-to-run noise on most cells.

Push delivers events at the wire-RTT floor (~3 to 5 ms) across producer rates from 100/s to 25k/s and consumer counts up to ~30, while polling adds roughly `poll_interval / 2` (about 55 to 80 ms at a 100 ms cadence, ~510 to 560 ms at 1 s). Push holds that floor as consumers scale: at N=100 with a constant 1k ev/s producer it stays at ~6 ms versus ~1270 ms for poll @ 100 ms (a ~200x gap). The N=100 collapse seen when fanout and rate scale together decomposes into three independent failure modes: a writer-side ceiling (100 single-row-INSERT backends cap near 16k ev/s, costing throughput, not latency), push CDC (100 wal-senders at >=10k ev/s exceed the host's WAL-out capacity, the only corner where push collapses), and poll CDC (100 polling clients each running `pg_logical_slot_get_binary_changes` ten times a second saturate PG's SQL backend on N alone, even at 1k ev/s). One caveat on terminology: "N consumers" here means N slots and N wal_senders, the worst case PostgreSQL constrains. A real deployment shares one slot and one engine, fanning out in process via `register(...)`, so it is bounded by predicate-VM throughput rather than by PG. Polling also generates 6 to 7x more committed PG transactions than push at N=100 under a fixed-rate workload.

WAL retention is per-slot, not global, so one stalled or crashed consumer does not pin WAL for the others (the "one bad consumer pins WAL for everyone" folklore only holds when many consumers share a single slot, which neither transport does here). Polling auto-advances its slot on every drain and sits near zero lag, whereas push retains WAL until the consumer calls `ack()` (about 5 MB accumulates over 90 s unacked, the deliberately-exposed worst case). Three regimes favor polling on per-event latency: large single-COMMIT batches that polling drains in one round-trip while push streams frame by frame, ack-undisciplined consumers where polling's auto-cleanup is a real safety property, and the push-saturated corner (N=100 at >=10k ev/s, where poll @ 100 ms collapses to ~1.5 s against push's ~4.7 s). The recommendation: default to push for real-time fan-out, where it wins decisively at every common cadence and up to ~30 consumers on a single PG instance. Polling remains a defensible alternative for bulk ETL or when ack discipline cannot be guaranteed, and the same library expresses both (`PollingPgCdcSource` implements the same `CdcSource` trait, so application code is portable). This study is single-host only. Multi-PG-node meshes, `proto_version >= 2` features, and disciplined-ack push behavior are out of scope (the mesh dimension is covered in the next section).

## On a PostgreSQL mesh (multiple PostgreSQL instances)

Push-only study, M in {1, 2, 4} Postgres instances on one Docker host, 5 consumers per cell (3 for the replication tree), 2 trials per cell.

The two fan-in geometries are equivalent at small M: G1 (one engine per PG, fan-in at the app layer) and G2 (`MultiSourceFanIn` ingesting many PGs into one logical consumer) stay within measurement noise of each other under uniform load, with cross-source skew under 0.5 ms through M=4. Aggregate drain falls below the `M x rate x N` ideal past M=2 (about 23% under at M=1 and M=2, 52% under at M=4), and shard fairness under unified fan-in stays near the ideal up to M=2 then drops at M=4. Both effects trace to a single-host writer-side saturation ceiling (the same one the single-PG writer-isolation experiment found), not to anything in the engine. Skewed load (one hot PG at 10k ev/s, the rest at 100) is the one regime where geometry choice is visible, but the noisy-neighbour penalty is sub-2 ms even in its worst cell. The sharpest finding is the replication tax: attaching a subql consumer to a PG-native logical-replication replica instead of the primary adds a ~130 ms apply-worker tax per replica, flat in both M and writer rate, moving the latency floor from ~3.7 ms to ~130 ms (a 35x tax for replica isolation).

For multi-PG deployments where each PG owns its tenant data: default to G1 (architecturally simpler, equivalent to G2 at small M, and it avoids the engine-level work a true G2 primitive would need); attach latency-sensitive consumers to the primary, not a replica; and do not stack M PG containers on one host, since the M=4 drain and fairness ceilings are genuine host saturation that a cross-host mesh would lift. Every saturation in this study reproduces a writer-side finding from the single-PG benchmark, so the mesh adds no new engine-side bottleneck. Out of scope: polling on a mesh, M > 4 and cross-host topologies, cross-instance event ordering, `proto_version 2` features, PG failover, and an engine-level "many sources per subscription" primitive (the G2 geometry here is composition over the intrinsically single-`DB` engine).
